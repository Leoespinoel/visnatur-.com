## 1. Where product ideas come from (00:01–00:10)

- 10 years as a footwear salesperson, then buyer, open to ~50 footwear brands quarterly. Customer questions were ~50% functional, 50% fashion, and that is where he learned *why* people like product.
- Taste was formed by volume: seeing 500 styles on a wall forces you to pick what you like most and have a point of view. "Seeing things for the first time and liking them for your own reasons" is a language he thinks has been lost; today familiarity (seeing it on social first) is the starting point.
- Ideas come from a mental catalog of everything he has seen. Example: a unique trail-runner colorway seen 20 years ago gets applied to a retro runner today. "Tweak and change things by 10% and it makes a huge difference within the category."
- He is "a footwear guy, not a sneaker guy." Brown shoes and boots matter as much as sneakers. First obsession: Dolomite boots, ~150 pairs, filled his parents' garage, no resale value, bought because they'd disappear next season.
- Saw the made-in-Italy boot era and made-in-England Clarks era up close: that calibrated his quality bar.
- 2,200 shoe styles in 15 years, and he personally worked on every one. "Never phoned in anything."

## 2. Who the customer is (00:08–00:10)

- Asked "are you the customer or is it strategic?" Answer: "It's me." The hardest thing to replicate about Kith is the single point of view / lens.
- Releases now draw lines in Korea, Japan, France, UK, Canada, NY, Miami, LA, Dallas, Chicago on the same day. He says he never takes that for granted.

## 3. Taste vs. style (00:10–00:17)

- "Taste and style are the two most important words in our world." His **taste has never changed; his style has evolved** with age (joggers then, baggy pants now; logo-heavy then, unbranded now).
- If your taste can be swayed by what you see on social, you don't have a strong point of view and your output won't be consistent.
- Consistency is what makes a brand easy to grow with: a "red thread" through everything. "The course has been additive."
- His stated job: "I'm just trying to build emotion. Transfer the emotion I have for the product to the people buying it."
- Kith's spectrum runs from Kith Treats (cereal, fun, young, graphic, monthly capsules, Junior's Cheesecake) to Giorgio Armani the next week. People who don't follow daily get confused and "hate what they don't understand." He has "never cared about anyone who hasn't put the time in."
- The brand is a welcoming space "for people who know the difference between great and good," at every price point and style.
- Licensing drops (Back to the Future etc.) are a small revenue driver. He does them because he loves film, music, sports. "It's everything I love."
- New York framing: the F train from 179th & Hillside shows you every style and culture in an hour. "We're a New York brand. I'll never be one thing or the other. I'll be everything that I am."
- Host's observation: new divisions track his life stage (Bugaboo stroller collab when his first child arrived; kids line).

## 4. The one project not from his passion: TaylorMade golf (00:18–00:21)

- Only Kith project that didn't start from his own passion. Refused the conversation for years. Did it because VP of product Michael (13–14 years with him) and COO Nick love golf and are "part of the cloth" of the company.
- Rule he applied: he would not put his ideas into it until he felt "studied enough." Built a huge mood board of everything that existed in golf hard goods, then designed from the outside in: all-black set, gradient metal pack.
- Took 1 to 1.5 years before release. Polarizing, but it brought people to golf product who never cared about golf. Now one of Kith's most successful sports releases and a lane people wait for.
- Principle: if your point of view can be impactful in a category you've researched, it becomes a new lane.

## 5. ASICS 2007 and choosing partners (00:21–00:24)

- First ASICS Gel Lyte III collab in 2007. Customers looked at the shoe like they didn't know what it was. That one project opened the inline Gel Lyte for a generation and widened ASICS' aperture beyond one silhouette.
- "Retro tech": products that were ahead of their time as performance gear come back as lifestyle. "Everything's cyclical."
- Host's advice for new brands: partner with tertiary / distressed heritage brands (Diadora, Reebok, Fila, Puma earlier) where the partnership is asymmetric and meaningful for both sides.

## 6. The archive (00:24–00:27, 01:54–01:56)

- Archivist Jordan laid out all footwear from day one in the long second-floor office. Almost every sample has been saved; 1,200 unreleased samples appear in the 15-year book.
- What struck him walking it chronologically: "how much product can evolve and still stay the same." Same silhouettes, radically different after 15 years.
- 15-year book: Assouline, their biggest/heaviest book ever. Every shoe re-shot by Tyler (OG photographer; "if Tyler wouldn't shoot it I wouldn't make the book"), ~8 months of shooting. Organised by brand, chronological. Back index shows every sample leading to each release; bold = released, non-bold = sample, sometimes none released. Exhibit called "Walk the Index."
- Only other founder he knows thinking in decade books: George from Represent.

## 7. Fear, risk, and the Lafayette bet (00:27–00:36)

- COVID: he genuinely thought Kith and physical retail would go to zero; people would stop caring about product.
- "When you start a brand there's so much of that feeling that it could all go to zero in a day." Surviving the scariest moments is what gets you over the hump.
- **The Lafayette story (told publicly for the first time):** Kith was at Broadway & Bleecker (expanded inside Atrium) with ~800 visitors/day, year 7 of a 10-year lease, very successful. But ~50% of traffic was irrelevant Broadway foot traffic that stopped staff building one-on-one relationships. He used to look up at 337 Lafayette (crumbling facade, eight businesses incl. a sweatshop, zero foot traffic north of Houston) and say "if I ever get this building, this is where I place the brand."
- Seven years later the call came. He paid to break the lease with "some of the first real money I got to see," moved to a standalone building with no traffic, and planned for 300 visitors/day: fewer people, better service.
- Build took a year longer (brick taken down and rebuilt to preserve the look). He was "petrified" about break-even. Second month open, traffic matched Broadway; then it surpassed it. "That was changing human behavior." He credits that global flagship with making Kith what it is.
- "You're going to need to be uncomfortable and you're going to need to throw up a few times. And I did."
- Support: his wife ("guardian angel") and Marcus Troy (friend since before Kith, keeps him honest, level-headed).

## 8. Self-funded (00:33–00:34)

- "I've never taken the chips off the table." Every profit share goes back into the company; that is the only way they got this big. Capex on stores and offices is substantial and entirely self-funded.

## 9. The hidden work behind "simple" product (00:36–00:43)

- Polished content makes it look easy; nobody sees ideation to completion. Chain: design direction (him) → designer → assistant designer → production → development → merchandising → logistics. Complexity affects timing and sample rounds, not creativity.
- Yankees sun-faded hoodie: **six samples** to get the fade non-blotchy, colour, embroidery, and fit right.
- **Fit lesson (industry "101"):** when a big brand's shirt doesn't fit you, it's often because the block (e.g. "the Henry") fit once, then they swapped fabric (oxford → wool, heavier weight) and never refit. "A big percentage of brands don't refit with different fabrics." Kith refits every SKU for its fabric.
- Ronnie was the men's fit model for 13–14 of 15 years, 3–4 hours a week. Stepped back from fitting everything only in the last year because it was eating everything else.

## 10. Pricing and value (00:43–00:49)

- Pricing is not top-down margin targets: **"Give people more than what they pay for. That's the motto."** If they could sell something for double, they won't just because they can.
- He still sits in the buy with Alex (merchandising), 4–5 hours a week, pricing every item against cost of goods.
- Consumers have a skewed idea of cost from fast fashion (quantity, fit, fabric). "A t-shirt is not a t-shirt and a hoodie is not a hoodie."
- Many Kith tees are made in Peru (Pima cotton, hand feel); some in China for washes. "China makes amazing product." Origin is a misconception; what matters is QC, fabric, construction.
- He spent three weeks in a Dominican Republic factory learning to make Sebago shoes station by station (stitching uppers, cementing, hand-sewn moc stitch). "I can make a shoe from A to Z." The problem is people who talk about product without knowing how it's made; today you can learn.
- A Tokyo store sells only white t-shirts in 1,000 different bodies. Kith & Kin (elevated line, pricier fabrics, fully unbranded) has five t-shirt fits in different weights. He obsesses over this.
- Host's tip: you can build your own t-shirt block starting from a manila-folder pattern; it's not that hard to begin.

## 11. Japan and collecting (00:49–00:53)

- Tokyo is his "happy place"; Junior (director of the Japan business, friend of 17–18 years) shows him around. Looking at buying an apartment there. Japan made him a "more purposeful collector."
- Still buys 6–8 pairs of early-2000s deadstock shoes on eBay a week (e.g. two Hiroshi HTM Air Force 1s). Kept in temperature-controlled storage units. Not for value, resale, or wearing: "it represents a time that helped build a culture."
- Wants to "bring substance back" to special footwear: community of like-minded people buying for the right reasons (for themselves). Unannounced projects coming that speak to that era.

## 12. Evaluating a collab or a brand (00:53–00:57)

- First things he looks at: is it true to the partner's DNA? Did you overshadow what made that brand special by over-embedding your own DNA? Over-design is the common failure. "My love for those brands needs to be evident in the product."
- Today: **~50% of why people buy is the product, ~50% is storytelling and content around it.** He personally buys 100% for product; everything else is a cherry on top.
- Mackis in Portland: didn't know 80% of the brands, still bought a lot. "I don't need to know the brand. I need the product to be incredible."
- Austin (long-time team member) describes go-to-market as feel and judgment, not a system.

## 13. What a purchase is meant to do; the new luxury (00:57–01:02)

- Goal 1: be **special**. Everything limited so the person next to you at work or school probably isn't wearing it. "Scarcity will always be a form of luxury," regardless of price. Watches and cars prove it.
- Luxury houses "got carried away" opening too many stores in too many cities and lost the scarcity that made customers feel special.
- **New luxury is emotion, not price.** A sequence of emotions: purpose for walking into the space → the product you gravitate to → the purchase → how you feel afterwards and how much use you get.
- Warmest compliment: people get asked about the pieces with no or tiny branding. He now wears mostly unbranded product (Kith & Kin).
- Goal 2: you own nothing that looks or feels like it. Goal 3: worth more to you than you paid. "Value is a word that's been destroyed by down-market retailers," but cost per wear is real and he wants people thinking about it *before* they buy anything, not just Kith.
- You don't have to buy: a $7–9 ice cream at Treats or a $20 sandwich at Ronnie's makes you part of the world. (Host calls this consumer surplus.)

## 14. No paid marketing (01:02–01:05)

- Why no paid ads? **"Because we don't have to."** He isn't interested in doubling revenue; he wants people to connect organically and create their own emotion, not have one "created for them" by an algorithm.
- Not a forever rule: if an authentic, organic format appears he'll research it.
- The real asset is **curiosity**: "The minute the curiosity goes away, we're going to be in a shitload of trouble." Many brands have lost consumer curiosity.
- Unpulled levers (host): paid, wholesale, department stores, off-price. He rejects the "you're screwed" framing: every brand has its own path; some must hit a number, some are passion-led. His purpose is "different from it needing to be about dollars and cents."

## 15. Spend on stores, not ads (01:05–01:10)

- Kith spends **$1,000–1,300 per sq ft** on a vanilla-box build-out, more than some luxury houses. He believes product would sell at the same rate on a quarter of that. His CFO is puzzled.
- Why: spaces that inspire, complement the product, and have "no ceiling in terms of aesthetic" so the product can evolve without feeling out of place. Spaces have gotten more minimal over time to stay open.
- Customers are emotionally invested in past store eras (the wood, the hanging Air Force 1s, the white crystal); they associate the space with what they bought. Host: "your stores are basically your website."
- Not a formula, a feeling. "The brand has a brain because it's been consistent for 15 years. I'm thinking for the brand." Decision-making must be "militant" but tasteful. He's in an 18–20-year mindset.
- People try to box you in based on what they've seen; anything beyond that confuses them until they experience it. The journey is dictated by purpose.

## 16. The Monday program (01:10–01:13)

- Something new every Monday for ~8 years, 52 times a year. Built as a malleable idea: often an appetizer (accessories/lifestyle portion ahead of a Friday collection), part rollout, part release strategy. Gives the audience one layer of predictability.
- Can be two hats, one tee, or a whole collection. The calendar is studied for bandwidth and the customer's journey through the year. "I want to do everything I can, not everything I should."
- Next year is planned by the week already. That lets content, editing, and experiential teams work with time. Product always ships late from brands, so sometimes he sells before stock arrives and ships 14 days later; one change dominoes across the year.
- About 80 people touch a release internally.

## 17. How a collab is built: BMW (01:13–01:19)

- Toby from BMW flew in and asked why he loved the brand: the E30 M3 was a poster on his wall; his grandfather had one. Later leased and spec'd a 330 (white, red interior).
- He wanted the E30 M3; BMW had an M4 coming. Story became past and present: the M3 two-door lineage becomes the M4. He put Johnny Cecotto (E30 M3 race driver) and his son on track in both cars.
- Apparel is the entry point for people who can't buy the car, or who want the car plus the world. The job is to transfer the knowledge and emotion he got in Germany. He compares it to forcing friends to hear a Foxy Brown freestyle on a mixtape: the same urge to put people on.
- At 44 he is in "legacy mode": making people happy. Standing outside the first Kith Treats window in Brooklyn watching every person smile after a bite mattered more than the store he'd spent so long building. **"The KPI is the feeling."**

## 18. Getting partners to "yes" (01:19–01:27)

- Partners say no to concepts that make them uncomfortable, often three or four times. Kith logo inside the BMW roundel: automatic no, then no, no, no. "Kith" in Coca-Cola script on a glass contour bottle: "a f***ing no."
- They come around when they understand the purpose: uplifting the partner's own DNA, not just making something new. "A big part of the Kith DNA is to disrupt." IP integration is central and must move the needle.
- Japan inspiration: the Japanese upspec American brands (a dollar can of Coke, Champion sweatshirts) into lifestyle with better storytelling, fit and make than Americans do for themselves. He wanted to do that for Coke: treat it as a lifestyle brand, the bottle as the base of the story, a world around it.
- Ambition: when you ask his customer what they think of first for Coca-Cola, they say the rugby, not the can. "What that does for a brand is priceless": it expands what the IP stands for.
- He is personally in every partner conversation because they need to hear the intent from the person creatively directing it. That's why relationships are long-standing.
- Kith Treats was originally going to be a white-chocolate bread window (story not finished on air).

## 19. Trips and organic content (01:27–01:31)

- Takes the same group of friends (no new names on the list in years, all successful in their own right) on trips: 25 people in matching ski kits taking over a mountain village in Hokkaido; taking over Amangiri and hiking in the product.
- **Nobody has ever been asked to post. No hashtags ("that shit's corny").** People post because they love it; that's human behaviour now. Influence comes from the scene itself.
- He is the connector among his friends; Kith is the platform that lets him see them, since he has no time otherwise.
- A 15-year event in the first/second week of October 2026 that he calls the biggest moment of his career and "a big moment in fashion."

## 20. Giorgio Armani: proudest collab (01:31–01:36)

- Armani was always the pinnacle: the lifestyle, the film characters. Mutual interest started 3–4 years before product shipped. Took a ton of education.
- He built a book for Mr. Armani and presented in person. Armani didn't love it at first; agreed once Ronnie walked through the purpose: paying respect through Giorgio's history, celebrating the archetype of every man Armani got into suits over 50 years (Armani turned 90).
- Host says it was Kith's biggest visible jump in maturity, the moment he'd buy Kith "in that vehicle." Ronnie: "That was the beginning of something new." Bergdorf Goodman was another such moment.

## 21. Knicks creative director (01:36–01:45)

## 22. What Kith has cost him (01:45–01:52)

- Would he sell? "Right now, no." Never says forever on anything; circumstances change. Thinks 3–5 years out, takes it a day at a time.
- Cost: **"Everything."** Weddings, birthdays, vacations, true friendships, and above all extended-family time (parents, sister, aunts, uncles, cousins). Done willingly: "I always have to maximize the potential of whatever I'm working on. If I'm sweeping the floor, the floor will be clean."
- Advice to the next generation: **devotion, work ethic, purpose.** "People are moving without purpose." Find a passion first, then move with purpose. Hardest part for young people is deciphering substance from noise.
- Host: nobody today will work 15 years in a stock room; everyone wants the fast track. Ronnie worries polished end results contribute to that and wants to show process more, but hated 18 months with a camera following him 12–15 hours a day.

## What applies to Vis Naturæ

Made-to-order, 10% of price to conservation, Europe, competing in the Aimé Leon Dore / Vilebrequin / Ralph Lauren space (and Fieg explicitly gives ALD his flowers for bringing a founder's life into the brand).

1. **One lens.** Every product and every page should pass through Leo's taste. Taste stays fixed; style and range can evolve. Write down the "red thread" and test each new piece against it.
2. **Scarcity is already built in.** Made-to-order means nobody next to you is wearing it. Say that plainly; it is a luxury signal independent of price.
3. **Price for value, not for margin.** "More than what they pay for" is a better pricing rule than target margin. Explain fabric, origin, construction and cost-per-wear on the product page; that is the storytelling half of the purchase.
4. **Fit per fabric.** When a block moves from cotton to linen or wool, refit it. This is the quality gap most brands leave open, and a made-to-order brand can own it.
5. **Know how it's made.** Fieg spent three weeks in the factory. The made-to-order process is itself the content: show the process, not just the polished result.
6. **No paid ads at launch; cultivate curiosity.** Launch with organic storytelling, a predictable rhythm (his Monday drop → a weekly release or story for Vis Naturæ), and real-world moments. Never ask anyone to post.
7. **Conservation is the emotional transfer.** The BMW/Coke lesson: teach people why you love the thing. Name the partners, tell their history, make the 10% feel like being let into a world rather than a checkout line item.
8. **Unbranded or small branding** earns the warmest compliment ("where did you get that?"). Keep logos small; let silhouette and fabric carry it.
9. **Destination over traffic.** His Lafayette move prioritised relationship over footfall. For a web-only brand that means a site people seek out, with service that feels one-on-one (fit guidance, made-to-order updates), not conversion tricks.
10. **Photography is the store.** Kith spends more on stores than luxury houses because the space is the website. For Vis Naturæ the imagery *is* the store; product photography deserves the budget placeholders are standing in for.
11. **Archive from day one.** Save every sample and sketch chronologically. The 10- and 15-year books only exist because he kept everything.
12. **Reinvest; think 3–5 years out, plan the calendar by week a year ahead.**
