    02 · The invitation

    Not a plan. An invitation.

    The site is built, the range is specified and the model is set. What it needs now is the people who complete it. This page is addressed to three of them, in no particular order.

    Co-founders

    A partner in product or operations, with as much creative input as the founder has. Someone who would rather make fewer things well.

    Investors

    No round is open and no figure is set. If editions, provenance and a fixed pledge interest you, the conversation starts before a round exists.

    Media

    Writers, photographers and filmmakers who want the story early, with the facts straight and the imagery labelled honestly.

    Still open, on purpose

    • The maker's numbers.Three quotes are pending from Mauritius: one piece, twelve pieces, and the pattern per design. Every figure on this page is a target until they land.
    • The partners.Three conservation remits are chosen. The organisations are in conversation and unnamed until agreements are signed.

    03 · The brand

    Style first. Never the other way round.

    Vis Naturæ is Latin for force of nature. It is a menswear and resort label in the register of Aimé Leon Dore, Vilebrequin and Ralph Lauren: swim, polos, linen, knit, a field jacket. The clothes earn the attention. The conservation is what people find when they look closer.

    • One taste lens.Every piece and every page passes through the founder's taste. Taste stays fixed. Style and range are allowed to move.
    • Scarcity, built in.Nobody next to you is wearing it. Twelve of each design exist, and the number is sewn in. That is a luxury signal independent of price.
    • Small branding.Silhouette and fabric carry it. The mark stays small. The warmest compliment is "where did you get that?"
    • Made for somewhere.Clothes made for the sea and the sun borrow everything from the places they are worn in. Ours are made on an island in the Indian Ocean.

    04 · The real challenge

    Two things that usually cancel out.

    Good clothes and conservation have rarely sat together without one cheapening the other. Holding both, without the cause tainting the brand, is the whole job.

    The trap on one side. Lead with the cause and we become a charity with merchandise. Nobody buys from a charity for the cut of the shorts.

    The trap on the other. Bury it and the ten percent becomes a footnote nobody feels. The one thing that makes us different disappears.

    The line we walk

    • Product gets the front row, always.Every drop, film and post is judged as clothing first. If it only works because of the mission, it does not go out.
    • Conservation is shown, not sold.The work and the people doing it, filmed well. No sad animals, no guilt, no asking.
    • One standard for both.The film about the reef gets the same director, grade and taste as the lookbook. The moment the conservation side looks cheaper, it taints everything next to it.
    • The number does the talking.Ten percent of the price, on every page, published every season. No campaign has to say it twice.

    05 · The problem

    The industry makes far more than it sells.

    Then it discounts, destroys or dumps the rest. We decided not to take part.

    We started with a question: what would a clothing brand look like if it refused to make anything nobody wanted? The answer was slower, and better. No stock. No sales. No seasons thrown away.

    The pieces are the ones we wanted and could not find. Swim shorts in a clean cut. A piqué polo heavy enough to hold its collar. Linen that is allowed to crease. A field jacket you will re-wax rather than replace.

    06 · The model

    Once.Twelve.Gone.

    1. DAY 0

      The window opens.

      Every design is released once. Buyers claim a number and choose a size. Nothing is cut yet.

    2. DAY 21

      The window closes.

      Numbers are fixed by order of payment. An edition of twelve with seven owners is made as seven. The other five are never made.

    3. WEEKS 3 TO 4

      One batch is made.

      Cut and sewn together in a small workshop in Mauritius, each piece in its owner's size, the unit number sewn in.

    4. 4 TO 7 DAYS

      It arrives.

      Express from Mauritius to the door. Duties may apply, and the site says so.

    5. THEN

      The design retires.

      It moves to the archive with the number of pieces that exist. The page stays live for good.

    • Accessories are editions of twenty-four.Caps, towels and totes.
    • Outerwear and heavy knit are one of one.Made first in one size, photographed, sold as that garment, dispatched within three working days.
    • Stripe Checkout is the only stock record.A checkout holds a number for thirty minutes, then releases it.
    • Prices are the same all year.No sale is ever run, which is what makes a numbered edition honest.

    07 · Why twelve

    One of one was the romantic answer. Twelve is the honest one.

    We started as a pure one-of-one brand. At an average price of about €174, a single sale left €30 to €60 before the design had been paid for. Covering a studio that way needs fifty to four hundred designs a month, or average prices near €550. Neither is this brand.

    Twelve amortises the pattern and the sampling across one small batch, keeps the piece rare, and keeps the price where a great product should sit. Outerwear stays one of one, where the price can carry it.

    09 · Where

    Made in Mauritius. By hand.

    An island in the Indian Ocean that has been making fine knitwear and tailoring for half a century. We work with small workshops we visit in person, and every edition is cut and sewn there as one batch.

    Express from Mauritius to Europe takes four to seven working days and costs us about €35 a parcel. A buyer pays at most €15, and nothing over €150. The difference is a cost we carry on purpose. It sits inside the margin targets.

    The making is the content. We show the process, not only the polished result.

    10 · The product rule

    Give people more than what they pay for.

    The photograph shows the outside only. The buyer discovers the rest on delivery. Inside the cap, inside the shirt, the waistband, the labels, the unit number. None of it is photographed or listed before purchase, so every piece arrives as more than what was bought.

    Every block is refitted for its fabric. A shirt that moves from cotton to linen is cut again, not just re-sewn. This is the quality gap most brands leave open, and a made-to-order brand can own it.

    What a buyer gets

    • A numbered piece, in their size, one of at most twelveThe number is fixed by order of payment and sewn in.
    • Details inside the garment the photographs never show
    • A free remake if the size is wrongWithin fourteen days of delivery.
    • Free repairs for life
    • No plastic in the packaging

    What we never do

    • Run a sale
    • Hold stock, or make a piece nobody asked for
    • Repeat a retired design
    • Buy an advertisement, or ask anyone to post
    • Photograph the inside before it is bought

    11 · Growth

    Built to sell out, on purpose.

    Twelve is small enough to sell out and large enough to pay for the pattern. A sold-out design is not a lost sale. It is the archive growing, and the reason the next window gets watched.

    "Scarcity will always be a form of luxury. Nothing to do with price."

    Ronnie Fieg

    • A predictable rhythm.Drops on a calendar, planned a year ahead, with a story released each week between them.
    • The archive is public from day one.Every retired design, with the number of pieces that exist. People collect what they can see the history of.
    • Pre-orders remove the risk.The edition is paid for before it is cut. No stock, no discounting, no landfill.
    • Destination, not traffic.A site people seek out, with service that feels one to one: fit guidance, remakes, repairs. No conversion tricks.
    • Each drop funds the next.Reinvest. Think three to five years out. No outside pressure to scale faster than the taste allows.

    12 · How it grows

    No paid ads. Curiosity instead.

    "Why no paid ads? Because we don't have to."

    Ronnie Fieg

    Half of why someone buys is the garment. The other half is the story around it. A web-only brand has no shop floor, so the imagery and the films get the budget a store would.

    • Earned first, then paid for.Everything goes out for free first. Only what already works gets any money behind it, and not at launch.
    • Never ask anyone to post.No hashtags, no seeding, no paid placements. People connect on their own terms.
    • The imagery is the store.Photography and film at the standard of the clothes. The reef gets the same director as the lookbook.
    • Archive everything.Every sketch and sample kept in order. The ten-year book only exists if we start now.

    14 · The pledge

    Ten percent of the price. Not the profit.

    Percent-of-profit pledges are easy to make and hard to check. Ours is on the sale price, before shipping and tax, shown on every product page, so anyone can do the sum. A €165 swim short sends €16.50 to conservation.

    Because the brand takes its name from the natural world, it pays rent.

    Pledged to date

    €0

    Counted from the first order. Updates as pieces are made and paid for. Transfers are published every season.

    15 · The mechanism

    From a checkout to a coastline.

    Transparency is the product feature. If we cannot show it, we do not claim it.

    1. 01

      A piece is bought.

      Ten percent of the sale price is set aside at checkout. Before costs, before margin, before shipping and tax.

    2. 02

      Held separately.

      Pledge funds sit in their own account. Never working capital, never payroll.

    3. 03

      Pooled each season.

      Allocated across the three remits: ocean, forest, rivers and wetlands.

    4. 04

      Transferred to partners.

      Established, audited organisations in each area, named before the first transfer is made.

    5. 05

      Published openly.

      Every season: raised, transferred, outcomes. Shown on the conservation page, where anyone can check it against the archive.

    16 · The ten percent

    No. It pays for itself.

    It does not come out of what is left. It does the work a marketing budget would, and it hands us the stories to tell.

    • It is priced in.The ten percent sits inside the price of every piece from day one, like the fabric. A cost we plan for, not money taken out at the end.
    • It is our marketing.Other brands pay to be noticed. The pledge is the reason people talk about us, share us and come back. It does the job ad money would.
    • It is our content.Every partner we fund is a place we can film. One place, one episode, and a run of clips cut from it. The seasonal numbers become posts in their own right.
    • It is the emotional transfer.The buyer is let into a world, not charged a line item. That is the difference between a pledge and a footnote.

    20 · Risks

    Six things, said out loud.

    Better said now than discovered six months in.

    • Risk 01 · the biggest one

      The maker's numbers come back high.

      Twelve pieces per design may not amortise the pattern the way the model assumes. The margin targets fall with it.

    • Risk 02

      Twelve do not sell.

      A window closes with three owners. The edition is made as three and the design still retires, but the pattern was paid for twelve.

    • Risk 03

      The product misses the bar.

      We call it great and the first piece is only good. The whole claim falls with it.

    • Risk 04

      Distance costs more than planned.

      Shipping loses €20 a parcel, duties surprise a buyer, a remake has to cross the ocean twice.

    • Risk 05

      The pledge loses trust.

      If we cannot show exactly where the money went, we become the problem this page describes.

    • Risk 06

      One founder, too many hats.

      Design, maker, site, films and the pledge all run through one person. Attention splits and the calendar slips.

    NextWhat do we do about them?

    21 · Mitigation

    One answer for each.

    • Answers 01

      Quote before launch, price after.

      Nothing opens until the three quotes are in. If twelve does not work, the edition size or the price moves, and the page says so.

    • Answers 02

      Pre-orders, and a smaller first run.

      Eight designs in Drop I, not eighteen. The window shows demand before anything is cut, and an under-sold edition costs one pattern, not stock.

    • Answers 03

      One piece at a time, no shortcuts.

      Refit every block per fabric. Release nothing until the sample holds up. The hidden details are the test: if the inside is not worth finding, it is not ready.

    • Answers 04

      Price the distance in.

      The €20 shipping gap sits inside the margin target. Duties are stated on every page. Sizing guidance and the remake policy are written to make the second crossing rare.

    • Answers 05

      Proof built in.

      Ten percent set aside at checkout, held in its own account, named partners before the first transfer, published every season.

    • Answers 06

      A seat beside the founder.

      This page exists to fill it. Product or operations, with real time and a real say. Until then, the calendar is sized to one person and says so.

    23 · The seats

    One seat taken, the rest open.

    Nobody is assigned a seat. Each person says what they can give and takes the one that matches. Every term below is a starting point, to be agreed in the room.

    The co-founder seat

    What it asks

    • Real time, alongside the founder
    • An area that is yours to run: product or operations
    • The same taste test on every piece

    What it gives

    • A founding share, earned over time so what you hold matches what you put in
    • An equal say in the brand and its direction

    The investor seat

    What it asks

    • Capital for samples, photography and the first drops, on terms agreed together
    • Acceptance that ten percent leaves every sale first
    • Patience with a brand built to stay rare

    What it gives

    • A stake in the company, on terms we agree
    • The same seasonal report the public sees: raised, transferred, outcomes

    The media seat

    What it asks

    • Your eye and your voice: the story, the imagery, the reach
    • Honesty about what is previsualisation and what is real

    What it gives

    • Early access to the editions, the atelier and the partners' places
    • A credit in the archive, which is public for good
